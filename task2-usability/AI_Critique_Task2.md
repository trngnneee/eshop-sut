The AI was useful for turning the assignment into a consistent research package, but its output also showed why a usability study cannot be delegated to a language model. It could inspect source code and notice likely defects, such as the contradiction between the profile’s example Vietnamese phone number and its validation rule. However, source inspection cannot tell whether real users will notice the same problem, how long they will hesitate, what recovery strategy they will try, or whether the logout state feels trustworthy. Treating those predictions as findings would create confirmation bias and fabricated evidence. The AI also initially considered a different shopping flow before I specified the account flow, showing that an underspecified prompt can produce a methodologically sound but irrelevant plan. After the flow was clarified, the AI still had to label all pre-session observations as provisional and keep participant names, contacts, quotes, times, ratings, and SUS responses empty. Its templates may also make data collection appear more objective than it is: moderator behaviour, think-aloud prompts, recruitment convenience, and prior knowledge from Task 1 can all influence seven small sessions. My main lesson is to use AI for structure, consistency checks, calculation, and traceability, while reserving observation and interpretation for evidence that I personally collect and verify. I must also test the protocol with a separate pilot, record every intervention, preserve raw SUS answers, and retain minority observations instead of forcing them into the AI’s expected themes. AI can improve discipline, but it cannot replace consent, neutral moderation, real participants, or accountable human judgement.
